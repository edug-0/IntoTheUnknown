About this view

SPHEREx Blink

What this is

SPHEREx Blink is our entry for the NASA Space Apps 2026 Planet X and SPHEREx challenge. You give it a position on the sky. It finds every SPHEREx image of that spot, lines them up and plays them as a short flip-book. Anything that moves or changes brightness between frames stands out against the fixed background stars.

Astronomers have "blinked" images like this for a century. Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto in 1930 by flipping between two photographic plates. SPHEREx gives this old method an infrared sky survey that covers the whole sky every six months.

What SPHEREx is

NASA's SPHEREx telescope launched on 11 March 2025. It maps the entire sky every six months in 102 infrared colours from 0.75 to 5.0 µm. Six detector arrays each sit behind a linear variable filter, so the wavelength changes smoothly across each array. As SPHEREx steps across the sky, a given star lands on a different part of the filter in each exposure. Together those exposures build up the star's full spectrum.

Four steps

  1. Pick a target. Click one of the five cards, or open "enter a name or coordinates". Names are looked up with CDS Sesame, which also fills in proper motion and parallax.
  2. Choose the data. Demo pack uses real SPHEREx cutouts that ship with the app and load instantly. Live IRSA fetches the newest data, which takes longer. Simulated is a synthetic sky that works offline.
  3. Press play space. Step with ← →. The dashed gold line is where the target should be, from its known motion. The gold cross-hair marks this frame's date.
  4. Explore. Switch to Difference to cancel out everything that doesn't change. Click a star to measure its spectrum. Open the light curve to see whether it varies.

Reading the picture

  • Mono: the selected detectors stacked into one image. North is up and east is left, like the sky seen from below.
  • 3-colour: blue = D1–D2 (0.75–1.6 µm), green = D3–D4 (1.6–3.8 µm), red = D5–D6 (3.8–5.0 µm). Cold brown dwarfs are red. Hot stars are blue-white.
  • Difference: orange means brighter than the reference and blue means fainter. A moving object leaves an orange/blue pair. A variable star glows one colour.
  • Frames: Season = one frame per six-month survey pass, which gives the best signal-to-noise. Visit and Exposure show faster changes. All stacks everything into one deep image.

What to look for in each target

Barnard's StarThe fastest-moving star in our sky, 10.4″ per year, which is 1.7 SPHEREx pixels. Between seasons six months apart it shifts visibly. Parallax adds a sideways wobble of ±0.55″. Its spectrum is a cool red dwarf's: brightest near 1–1.6 µm.
ε Indi BA pair of brown dwarfs (T1 + T6) orbiting the star ε Indi A, 12 light-years away. Moves 4.7″/yr. Methane absorbs light at 1.6, 2.2 and 3.3 µm, so its spectrum has sharp peaks and dips.
WISE 0855The coldest known brown dwarf, about 285 K, which is close to room temperature. It is nearly invisible below 3.8 µm, so view it with detectors D5–D6 (the app does this for you). At 8.1″/yr it is the fastest mover of the five.
RW AurigaeA young T Tauri star still wrapped in its dusty birth disc. It dims and brightens irregularly as dust passes in front of it. Its spectrum has an infrared excess beyond ~2 µm from warm dust.
R DraconisA Mira variable, a pulsating red giant with a 246-day period. It changes brightness a lot between SPHEREx seasons, more at short wavelengths. Water absorption shapes its spectrum.

Hunting for something new

A distant, undiscovered planet ("Planet X") would move only a few arcseconds per hour. Over a few days it crosses several SPHEREx pixels. Use Exposure frames with a larger field of view, then Difference. Anything leaving an orange/blue trail that is not a known Gaia star is worth a second look. The simulated sky includes one such slow mover so you can practise.

Where the data comes from

  • Demo pack (demo/): real SPHEREx Level-2 spectral-image cutouts downloaded from NASA/IPAC IRSA by tools/fetch_demo.py. Each file keeps its full WCS, flags and per-pixel wavelength table, so the analysis is exactly the same as in live mode.
  • Live IRSA: an ADQL query to IRSA's spherex.plane/spherex.artifact tables, then cutouts from IRSA's cutout service.
  • Overlays: ESA Gaia DR3 and AllWISE via CDS VizieR. Aladin Lite and Sesame by CDS Strasbourg.

How images are processed

  1. Subtract the zodiacal-light model (ZODI) and each image's median sky.
  2. Resample every cutout through its own TAN-SIP WCS onto one north-up 6.15″/pixel grid, because SPHEREx rolls between visits.
  3. Combine the chosen detectors in each frame (median, mean or sum), then stretch for display.
  4. Photometry: a 2.2-pixel aperture with a 4–7-pixel sky annulus, converted from MJy/sr to µJy. An optional aperture correction uses a Gaussian PSF with the width given in each file's PSF_FWHM header, integrated over each pixel.

How the simulated sky is built

Simulated mode is clearly labelled. It exists so the tool works offline and so you can check the analysis against a known answer. It uses:

  • the target's real proper motion and parallax (the same code that predicts the path overlay);
  • the real survey cadence: SPHEREx looks 90° from the Sun, so a spot is seen when the Sun is 90° from it in ecliptic longitude. That gives two windows a year near the ecliptic and near-continuous coverage at the ecliptic poles. Only dates since the survey began (1 May 2025) up to today are used;
  • all six detectors exposed at once, with wavelengths spread along each linear variable filter (R ≈ 41 for D1–D3, 35 for D4, 110–130 for D5–D6);
  • model spectra built from blackbodies plus the main molecular bands for each object type, and realistic variability: Mira pulsation with a 246-day period, and irregular dust dimming for RW Aur;
  • field stars whose density depends on Galactic latitude, and one slow distant solar-system body.

The spectrum tab draws the input model as a dashed line in simulated mode. The measured points should fall on it, which checks the photometry and wavelength code from start to finish. PSF width and single-exposure depths are approximations, not calibrated values.
